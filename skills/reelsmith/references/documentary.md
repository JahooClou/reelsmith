# Documentary editing: principles for real-people reels

Read this before cutting anything built from observed reality: news, humanitarian work,
events, portraits. The reel is a documentary first and a vertical video second.

It distils the standard documentary texts and editors' own accounts, with sources at
the end. It was written after a documentary cameraman rejected a technically clean cut
as "no story, no emotions, just a rough edit of clips". Everything below exists to keep
that from happening again.

---

## 1. Story before shots

- **Find the question first.** Before touching the timeline, write one sentence: who
  wants what, what changed, what stands in the way, what could be lost (Bernard;
  Everett). If you can only describe a striking shot or a topic, you do not have the
  story yet.
- **The film you planned is not the film in the footage.** Kim Roberts: there is the
  film you plan, the one you shoot and the one you edit, and they differ. Log first and
  let the material tell you.
- **The editor writes.** In vérité there is no script, so structure is authored in the
  cut (Demetrakas; Roberts). Work in drafts. Oppenheim expects many, and seven is a
  minimum, not a luxury.
- **Stuck?** Start from a scene that already cuts smoothly and says something
  (Demetrakas). If you catch yourself shuffling clips around, stop and name the
  through-line (Hawke).

## 2. Structure: escalating peaks, not a travelogue

- Three acts work as a **diagnostic, not a template** (Bernard; Rabiger).
  - Act I sets the world and the disruption.
  - Act II brings complications that **change** the situation. Do not stack similar
    incidents.
  - Act III is the true outcome, and its consequence.
- **Everett's Doc Plot Map** puts emotional peaks at roughly 24% (end of Act I), 54%
  (midpoint), 80% (Act II climax) and 95% (final climax). Follow each peak with a
  quieter passage so it can land. The peaks escalate: each should hit harder than the
  one before.
- **Geography or chronology is not structure.** "Border, then station, then city" is a
  route. Order material by emotional escalation, and reorder freely so long as the
  reorder makes no factual claim the footage cannot support.
- **A mosaic of many people is legitimate** (Amend: interweaving has no formula; it is
  trial and error). But each strand still needs a beat and a direction.

## 3. The opening

- The **point of attack** (Bernard) is where the viewer can grasp a live question, an
  action or a relationship. A cold open must promise the kind of film that follows, not
  bait with an unrelated shock.
- In short form the hook is the first 1–3 s. Open on a specific human moment or a
  concrete action, and make it readable **without sound**. Never open on a logo, a
  generic establishing shot or an unexplained number.
- Show the film's best production values early, and establish any unusual device
  (text cards, sound-ups, speed changes) early so later uses feel intentional
  (Everett).

## 4. Cutting: emotion first

- **Murch's rule of six** ranks what a cut must preserve: emotion 51%, story 23%,
  rhythm 10%, eye-trace 7%, 2D screen plane 5%, 3D space 4%. It is a ranking, not a
  formula. When they conflict, keep the emotion and sacrifice continuity.
- **Shot length follows attention, not a grid** (Rabiger). Hold a face while the viewer
  is still reading it. Cut when attention moves.
- **Do not cut every shot on the beat.** Mechanical beat-cutting reads as monotony
  (Everett). Vary it: a run of 2-beat cuts, then a held shot through 8 beats. Holding
  against the pattern is what makes the pattern felt.
- **Portrait shots** are quiet close-ups where the viewer imagines someone's inner world.
  They are the emotional currency of observational film (Everett). Amend looks for
  faces she can hold, and eyes looking back.
- **Juxtaposition makes meaning** (Rabiger). Know what each adjacent pair implies, and
  never let a pair assert cause or simultaneity that did not happen.

## 5. Sound is half the picture

- **Natural sound is not decoration** (Poynter). It takes the viewer there, and it can
  tell the story faster than words.
- The TV-news practice (the NPPA style) uses nat sound as:
  - action and reaction
  - an attention-grabber
  - an adverb
  - punctuation
  - a location change at the head of a scene
  - rhythm
  - Always relevant to the picture (Edit Foundry).
- **J- and L-cuts:** let a sound lead into or trail out of a picture. They stitch
  scenes, and good editors use them liberally (Everett). Pre-lap the next scene's sound.
- **Music restraint:** first try the strongest vérité moment with no score. Amend
  avoids music under vérité and interviews because they carry their own power and
  music can feel manipulative. Save music for montage or structural passages.
- **Use music found in the footage.** If someone sings or plays in the material, build
  on it (Amend: the inmate's "Danny Boy" on the flute became the end credits of
  *Asylum*). Diegetic song beats library score for truth.
- **Silence** can outperform narration or music in a dramatic moment (Everett).

## 6. Text on screen

- **One job per card:** identify, bridge, state one fact, or give an outcome. Roughly
  3–8 words per line. Hold it long enough to be **read twice** (Everett).
- **Never on a face or on the key action.** Keep it inside the platform safe area
  (about the central 75% of the height). BBC places vertical subtitles in the central
  90% × 75%.
- **Statistics:** check the source, date, scope and what is actually counted (entries
  or people? estimate or count?). Put a small source line on the card itself (BBC
  guidelines; Reuters standards).
- **The last card goes on black or on a non-face image**, after the picture has
  finished speaking.

## 7. Endings

- End on the truthful change, not a compulsory triumph. Kim Roberts rejected endings for
  *Unrest* that were either falsely happy or too pitying, and let pictures and music
  carry the nuance.
- Keep the denouement short: a breather and a glimpse of life after (Everett). No new
  conflict, no long rumination.
- A loop is optional and never a substitute for an ending.

## 8. Dignity: non-negotiable with refugees and crisis footage

- **Suffering is not a shortcut to emotion.** Ask of every shot: does it help the viewer
  understand a person and a situation, or does it only make them feel something at
  that person's expense? Poverty porn and misery porn fail this test (Dóchas Code;
  UNHCR).
- **Children:** do not use a child's distress as the emotional payload. Prefer care,
  play or agency, such as a hand held, a child eating or a hat pulled straight, over
  crying close-ups. If crying is used, it must be held within care (a parent's embrace)
  and not be the last word.
- **Agency:** show people doing things, like carrying, deciding, writing signs, singing
  and helping each other, not only receiving aid. Avoid **saviour framing**, where the
  helpers or the host country are the heroes and the displaced are passive (Dóchas;
  Radi-Aid).
- **Identification and safety:** consider whether showing a face could harm someone,
  and in particular men who fled against a travel ban, or people with family still in
  danger.
- **Politicians and campaigns:** keep them out unless the story is about them. Real
  suffering must not become campaign imagery.

## 9. Process checklist for a documentary reel

1. **Log everything,** picture and sound. Mark moments that move you, portrait shots,
   action beats and nat-sound events. Listen to production audio, not only the frames.
2. **Write the question and the through-line in one line each.** Then sketch the peak
   map (24/54/80/95%).
3. **Make a bulky assembly** with sync sound only, no music. Does it already carry
   feeling? If not, the structure is wrong, and music will not fix it.
4. Add music as a layer, then decide:
   - where it **drops out** for nat sound or silence
   - where **found music** can take over
   - where the cuts **break** the beat grid
5. Cards: one job each. Check reading time, sources, and that none sits on a face.
6. **Dignity pass:** check every child, every tear, every identifiable face, and every
   helper-versus-helped framing.
7. **Watch it muted,** then eyes closed. Does the picture tell it alone? Does the sound
   tell it alone?
8. Show the cut to someone fresh. Note where they lean in and where they drift. Revise.
